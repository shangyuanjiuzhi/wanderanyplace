Three Generations, Ninety Years: How the Leshan Giant Buddha Was Carved

It was not built for faith alone, but to tame a river. From 713 to 803, three generations cut an entire mountain into a Buddha.

Introduction

The Leshan Giant Buddha is remarkable not only because it is 71 metres tall, but because of why it exists: to protect people from a river. It is also not a statue that was assembled stone by stone. It was subtracted from Lingyun Hill — cut directly out of the red sandstone. In 1996, it was inscribed with Mount Emei as a UNESCO World Cultural and Natural Heritage Site.

Stand at the water's edge and you see a head, shoulders, hands and feet. Behind them lies nearly a century of relayed effort. The project began in 713, in the first year of the Tang dynasty's Kaiyuan era, and was not finished until 803. The monk who started it never saw the finished Buddha. Neither did the governor who resumed it. The man who completed it was the third major figure in the chain.

This guide follows the timeline first — who did what, and when — then unpacks the engineering shown in the site's explanatory panels: why red sandstone was both a gift and a problem, where the drainage system actually hides, and why the Nine-Turn Plank Road always has a queue. It ends with tickets, transport and a walking route, so you can read the story again on site.

The short answer

The Leshan Giant Buddha began as a flood-control project, not a purely religious one. It was carved out of the mountain rather than attached to the cliff. And what has kept it alive for more than 1,300 years is a drainage system cut into the rock itself — through the hair coils, behind the ears, along the robe and deep into the shoulders and back.

1. Origin: A Vow to Calm the River

Leshan sits where the Min, Qingyi and Dadu rivers meet. At the foot of Lingyun Hill the three currents collide, the water accelerates and eddies form. For wooden boats, this was not scenery. It was a daily hazard, and crossings could end in disaster.

In 713, the monk Haitong of Lingyun Temple watched boats capsize and decided to carve a Buddha into the cliff. His reasoning was practical. The Buddha's power would calm the water, and the enormous quantity of rock removed could be tipped into the river to change the current and make the channel safer. From the outset, this was a hydraulic project as much as a religious one.

Haitong travelled around to raise funds, but local officials demanded a cut. He refused. According to the stele later written by Wei Gao, he told them, "You may gouge out my eyes, but you will not take the Buddha's money," and is said to have gouged out his own eye on the spot. The details are impossible to verify, but the story explains why the Buddha became a symbol of protection for ordinary people.

2. Ninety Years, Three Generations: Who Built the Leshan Giant Buddha?

The project spanned four reigns of the Tang dynasty and took about 90 years from first cut to final stone. No single person or generation could finish it. Laid out as a timeline, it looks like a relay race: one man starts, another revives it, a third completes it.

Haitong: the founder and the first pause

Haitong was the project's first leader and handled the hardest part: going from nothing to something. He chose the site, raised the money, organised the craftsmen and fixed the scale of the statue. Work had reached the shoulders when he died, and the project stopped. Without him there would be no Buddha; but he never saw it finished.

Zhangqiu Jianqiong: the man who paid from his own salary

After Haitong's death the work stalled twice. Zhangqiu Jianqiong, then military governor of Jiannan Xichuan, donated his salary to restart it. A regional commander willing to spend his personal income on a religious construction project tells us something: by then the Buddha was seen as public infrastructure, not just a temple's business.

Wei Gao: the man who set the last stone

The project was finally completed under another governor, Wei Gao. He oversaw the legs, the lotus base and the finishing works, and in 803, when it was done, he wrote the stele inscription. Much of what we know about the construction, the people and parts of the design comes from that record.

The detail most visitors miss

After the Buddha was finished, a 13-storey pavilion was built over it during the Tang and Song periods. It was known as the Great Image Pavilion (Daxiang Ge) in the Tang and the Tianning Pavilion in the Song. The structure sheltered the statue and gave worshippers a covered route around it. It was destroyed in war, and only then did the Buddha become fully exposed to wind and rain. That is why later repairs focused so heavily on drainage and surface weathering.

3. How It Was Carved: Subtracting a Buddha from a Mountain

Many visitors assume the Buddha was carved onto the surface of the cliff. In fact it was cut out of the red sandstone of Lingyun Hill. The craftsmen worked from the top down, level by level: first the rough silhouette, then the details — face, robe folds, fingers and toes.

This method had two advantages. The workers always stood above the carving, so they did not need scaffolding covering the whole cliff. And by removing large blocks first and refining later, they reduced the risk of a sudden collapse. In other words, the construction sequence was also a safety system.

There was also a clever optical decision: the head was deliberately enlarged. Visitors and boats look up from below, and perspective makes a head appear smaller. By scaling it up, the craftsmen made the statue look balanced and dignified from the river. The proportions that seem "just right" today were calculated for a viewing angle more than a thousand years ago.

4. The Hidden Drainage System That Kept It Alive for 1,300 Years

Red sandstone was both the Buddha's gift and its enemy. It is soft enough to carve, which is why the Tang craftsmen could attempt a statue of this size. But it is also porous. Leshan is wet, and once water enters the rock it brings moss, plant roots and surface decay. Without drainage, a statue this size would have crumbled long ago.

The ancient solution was to cut the drainage into the statue and the mountain at the same time. There are no exposed pipes. Instead, the builders used the structure of the body and the slope of the hill to move water away in stages. Think of it as four lines of defence:

1. The hair coils

The head has 18 tiers of coiled hair. Horizontal drainage channels were cut into the 4th, 9th and 18th tiers, so rain runs off the crown instead of soaking into it.

2. Behind the ears

Hidden holes behind the ears feed water into channels inside the rock. This diverts the flow without letting it scour the ears or the face.

3. The collar and robe folds

The folds of the robe were designed as gutters. Rain follows the collar and the drapery down and is guided to the sides of the body instead of running across the chest.

4. Shoulders, chest and back

Hidden galleries and chambers inside the shoulders, chest and back collect seepage and lead it out through the cliff face.

How to read this image

The panel cuts the head open and traces the water in blue: first the small holes in the hair coils, which feed hidden grooves across the scalp; then the holes behind the ear, which send water into the mountain; finally the continuous channels in the head, shoulders and back, which discharge it at the sides. The inset at lower left is a close-up of the opening inside the ear.

The blue lines are explanatory, not surviving exposed pipes. Most of the real system is buried under weathering and moss, and some channels can only be recognised at close range. The "traces of time" label matters too: the vertical streaks on the body are both evidence of weathering and evidence that the drainage has been working for centuries.

The cleverest part is that the system is part of the mountain. The channels were not bolted on later; they were cut into the rock. Their slope, direction and outlets all follow the structure of the hill. As long as the mountain itself does not collapse, gravity keeps the system working. That is why most later repairs were not about replacing pipes, but about clearing silt, sealing cracks, stabilising rock and protecting the surface.

Between 2018 and 2019, the site carried out a systematic survey and emergency conservation project, focusing on weathering, hollowing and seepage around the face, chest and abdomen. Work of this kind follows the principles of minimal intervention and retaining historic character — the aim is not to repaint the Buddha to look new.

5. The Nine-Turn Plank Road: Seeing the Buddha at Eye Level

There are two basic ways to see the Buddha: from the mountain and from the river. The mountain experience centres on the Nine-Turn Plank Road down to the platform at the Buddha's feet. The walkway is built against the cliff, zigzagging down in nine bends over roughly 500 metres, and is the main route between the ridge and the river.

At the bottom, the scale changes completely. The statue is 71 metres tall — more than 20 storeys — and the top of the foot alone is about 8.5 metres across, wide enough for a dozen adults to stand side by side. Photographs make it look manageable. Standing at the feet does not.

How to read this image

The panel places three ways of looking in the same scene. On the left, the plank road is the "walk down and look up" option. Below, the platform at the feet is the "stand underneath" option. On the river, the cruise boat is the "step back and see the whole silhouette" option. The vertical scale repeats the 71-metre height, and the red rock face explains why the path has to hug the cliff: there is no flat ground outside it.

It also explains a practical reality. Why is the plank road always one-way, and why does it always queue? Because it cannot be widened. There is only so much room on the cliff, and in peak season the only safe solution is flow control.

The queue, honestly

In peak season and on public holidays, the Nine-Turn Plank Road can take one to three hours. The route runs one way; when the plank road is under maintenance, visitors are diverted to the longer but gentler Lingyun Plank Road. If you do not want to spend half a day in line, enter at opening time or hike late in the afternoon, and book your ticket online in advance.

6. A Century in Four Photographs: Weathering and Repair

The Buddha has not looked the same since the day it was finished. Once the pavilion was gone, rain, humidity, moss and plants began to change its surface. Four headshots — 1909, 1929, 1996 and 2019 — happen to form a timeline of that change.

In the 1909 and 1929 images, the head is surrounded by vegetation and heavy weathering; the features are blurred and the face is marked by water stains and loss. By 1996 the outline is much clearer, though vertical streaks are still visible on the cheeks and neck. The 2019 image shows the face, brows and hair coils fully legible after systematic conservation.

How to read this comparison

The four photographs were taken from different angles, with different exposure and different vegetation around the head. They are not a simple damage scale. But they do tell us three things: the face was exposed to water and plants for a long time; a major repair had already taken place before 1996; and the 2018–2019 project brought the details back into view.

The point is not which photo looks better. It is that a stone monument is not a finished object. It survives through continuous care. The Leshan Giant Buddha is still here because Tang drainage, later repairs and modern monitoring have all done their part.

7. Visiting the Leshan Giant Buddha: Tickets, Route and Timing

Tickets and booking

Admission is about 80 yuan; the river cruise is about 70 yuan. Both can change, so check the official notice. Book online in advance with your passport or ID, especially in peak season and around public holidays.

Opening hours

Roughly 07:30–18:30 in peak season, with an earlier close in low season. Hours shift with the season, so confirm before you go.

Getting there

High-speed trains from Chengdu to Leshan take about an hour; from Leshan Station, a bus or taxi reaches the North Gate in around 20 minutes. A private car from Chengdu can also combine Leshan with Mount Emei.

Best time

The first hour after opening, or after 16:00. Morning light is better for the head; late afternoon is quieter on the river. Avoid National Day, Spring Festival and summer weekends if you can.

The official walking loop runs roughly like this: North Gate → Lingyun Temple → head viewing platform → Nine-Turn Plank Road down to the feet → Lingbao Pagoda → East Gate. Allow three to four hours, including queues and photos. The hand-drawn map below marks the walking time for each section.

Budget three to four hours in practice. If the plank road queue is too long, visit Lingbao Pagoda first and come back to the head platform for river views. With elderly visitors or small children, skip the descent and take the boat; it is far less tiring.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who built the Leshan Giant Buddha, and how long did it take?

The monk Haitong of Lingyun Temple began it in 713. After his death, Zhangqiu Jianqiong donated his salary to continue it, and Wei Gao oversaw the completion in 803. The project took about 90 years.

Why was the Leshan Giant Buddha built?

It began as a flood-control project. Leshan sits at the confluence of the Min, Qingyi and Dadu rivers, where boats frequently capsized. Haitong wanted the Buddha to calm the water and planned to use the excavated rock to change the current.

How tall is the Leshan Giant Buddha?

About 71 metres, with a head around 14.7 metres, ears about 7 metres, shoulders about 24 metres wide and a foot about 8.5 metres across. It is the largest surviving stone-carved seated Maitreya Buddha in the world.

Why has it survived for more than a thousand years?

The red sandstone is easy to carve but porous. The builders cut drainage channels into the hair coils, behind the ears, along the robe and through the shoulders, chest and back, leading water out through the cliff. Later repairs and the Tang-Song pavilion also helped.

Is the Nine-Turn Plank Road worth it, and how long is the queue?

It is the only walking route to the feet of the Buddha and the best way to feel its scale. Queues of one to three hours are common in peak season; the route is one-way, and maintenance can divert visitors to the Lingyun Plank Road. Arrive at opening time.

How much is the ticket, and how do I get there?

Admission is about 80 yuan, and the river cruise about 70 yuan; check the official notice. High-speed trains from Chengdu reach Leshan in about an hour, then a bus or taxi takes around 20 minutes to the North Gate.

Should I hike or take the boat?

Hike to see the feet, the plank road and Lingyun Temple up close, but expect stairs and queues. Take the boat for the full panorama with less effort, but you cannot go ashore. With a full day, do both.

Conclusion

The story of the Leshan Giant Buddha is, at heart, a story of relay. Haitong began it, Zhangqiu Jianqiong revived it, Wei Gao finished it. Tang craftsmen cut the body; later generations maintained the drainage; modern conservators monitor and repair it. Ninety years to build a Buddha, thirteen centuries to keep him seated. Next time you stand at his feet, look down at the rock as well as up at the face. The unremarkable grooves and holes are the real protection.
