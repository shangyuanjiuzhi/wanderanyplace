One Bronze Statue, One Ming Hall: Reading 1,600 Years of Mount Emei

At Wannian Temple, the statue and the hall that shelters it are more than six centuries apart. A "thousand-year-old temple" is rarely one building — it is layer upon layer of rebuilding.

Introduction: Look at the Statue, Then the Hall

At Wannian Temple on Mount Emei, two objects in the same room are separated by more than six centuries. The bronze Samantabhadra riding an elephant was cast in 980, during the Northern Song. The domed hall around it was built in 1600, under the Ming. Look up at the ceiling, then down at the statue, and you are standing between two dynasties.

This is not an exception. It is the key to reading the mountain. A "thousand-year-old temple" is rarely built in one go. It grows like a tree: the first monastery is the root, later reconstructions are the rings, and today's Golden Summit is new growth. Each generation adds something and covers something else.

This guide follows the timeline from 399 to 2006 through six illustrated panels. How did Buddhism enter the mountain? Why did Samantabhadra become its central figure? Why are the statue and its hall different ages? Why should you stop and listen at Qingyin Pavilion? And how should you read the four wonders of the summit? Once you see the layers, you are no longer looking only at scenery.

The short answer

Mount Emei is not a single "ancient mountain" but a place that keeps being rewritten. The temple site of 399, the bronze of 980, the brick hall of 1600 and the new statue of 2006 represent faith, craft, architecture and contemporary building. Once you notice the gaps between their dates, you are reading the mountain correctly.

1. 399: How Buddhism Entered the Mountain

In 399, the Eastern Jin monk Hui Chi travelled into Shu to spread the Dharma and was drawn to Mount Emei. Later gazetteers record that he founded Puxian Temple here — the predecessor of today's Wannian Temple. This is the most frequently cited starting point for the mountain's Buddhist history.

It was not an easy beginning. Reaching Mount Emei from the Sichuan basin meant a long mountain road, with scriptures, statues and food carried on someone's back. A monk first needed a place to stay, then a temple, then an audience, then disciples. Buddhism took root in the mountains not through one founding act, but through people who kept arriving.

How to read this comic

The page has three tiers, matching three steps of arrival. At the top, the monks are still walking: the older one carries the scriptures and tells his disciple, "The road is still long." In the middle, a temple is being raised: workers lift beams and call, "Steady the beams." At the bottom, the monk sits in a covered walkway and invites villagers, "Please sit, listen to the sutra."

Notice what the comic does not show: miracles. It shows people walking, lifting timber and listening. That is the most honest part of Mount Emei's history. A sacred mountain is not declared in a day; it is made by monks, craftsmen and believers over generations.

2. Why Samantabhadra? How a Mountain Became a Sacred Site

China's four sacred Buddhist mountains form a set of correspondences: Wutai Mountain with Manjushri, Putuo with Guanyin, Jiuhua with Ksitigarbha, and Mount Emei with Samantabhadra (Puxian). But these pairings were not fixed from the beginning. They were recognised, argued and accepted over time.

On Mount Emei, three traditions reinforced one another: early temples that worshipped Samantabhadra, monks who taught the tradition continuously, and pilgrims who identified the mountain with the "Great Radiance Mountain" described in scripture. Later imperial support and centuries of temple building deepened the link. By the time "Puxian's sacred site" became common usage, it was already a tradition maintained by many generations.

A daochang is more than a temple. It is a place of practice, teaching and pilgrimage. People come not only to look at buildings but to complete a journey. That is why Mount Emei's religious life is also a form of local identity.

How to read this comic

The first tier is recognition: a monk unfolds a painting of Samantabhadra on a six-tusked white elephant and says, "This is the identification of the faith." A listener replies, "So it is connected to Samantabhadra." The second tier is pilgrimage: the monk points to the temple and says, "A sacred site is a place of practice," while travellers arrive with packs. The third is construction: craftsmen steady the elephant base of a statue.

Together they show how a sacred site is made: scripture supplies the basis, monks interpret it, pilgrims vote with their feet, and craftsmen turn it into something visible. "Puxian's sacred site" is the result of that whole chain, not a single decree.

3. Wannian Temple: A Statue and a Hall From Different Centuries

Wannian Temple is where Mount Emei's layers are easiest to see. The temple has changed names as well: it began as Puxian Temple, was later called Baishui Temple, and only in the Wanli era of the Ming became Wannian Temple. Today two works from very different periods share one room.

The bronze Samantabhadra riding an elephant was cast in 980, the fifth year of the Northern Song's Taiping Xingguo era. It is about 7.35 metres tall and weighs around 62 tonnes, with Samantabhadra seated on a six-tusked white elephant. This is not a replica. It has been worshipped here since the Song dynasty.

The hall around it is younger — much younger. The Beamless Brick Hall was built in 1600, in the 28th year of the Wanli era, more than six centuries after the statue. "Beamless" means the main structure does not use a timber roof frame; brick vaults and a dome carry the weight. Look up at the dome, then down at the bronze, and two dynasties sit in front of you.

Object Date What to look for
Puxian Temple (predecessor) c. 399 The starting point of Mount Emei's Buddhist community
Bronze Samantabhadra 980 (Northern Song) Scale, casting technique and the six-tusked elephant
Beamless Brick Hall 1600 (Ming) How brick vaults and the dome carry the roof without timber beams
Golden Summit statue 2006 How contemporary building continues the Samantabhadra tradition

Note: the 980 date follows the standard account. Dimensions and weight are commonly cited figures; check the on-site object label for the latest information.

How to read this comic

Two lines of dialogue carry the main idea: "First look at the statue" and "The hall is several centuries later." The third tier adds a structural cutaway. A monk points to the dome and says, "This is supported by brick and stone," while a craftsman carrying timber asks, "Where are the wooden beams?" There are none. The roof is carried by rings of brick vaulting that transfer the load to the walls.

That is why "beamless" deserves attention. It is not decoration; it is a structural choice. Without timber beams, the building reduces its worst fire risk, and the thick brick walls and dome also suit the protection of a very large bronze statue. The design solved structure and conservation at the same time.

4. Qingyin Pavilion: Where the Landscape Has a Voice

Walking down from Wannian Temple, slow down at Qingyin Pavilion. The site was originally called Niuxin Temple. When it was rebuilt in the Ming dynasty, it was renamed after a line by the Jin poet Zuo Si: "No strings or bamboo are needed; mountains and water have their own clear sound." You do not need an orchestra. The landscape is the music.

The layout is unusual. The Black Dragon and White Dragon streams meet here, known together as the Black and White Waters; two bridges cross them, Niuxin Pavilion stands on the high ground between, and the water swirls around Niuxin Stone. The bridges are for the eyes, but the sound of water ties the whole forest together. Stand in the pavilion and listen before you take a photo.

This is another layer of Mount Emei's history that visitors often miss: naming. Calling a place "Clear Sound" tells us how earlier generations wanted us to experience it — not by measuring the height of the mountain, but by hearing how water moves through stone.

How to read this comic

The first tier explains the name: a monk holds a plan while the caption notes that Qingyin Pavilion was once Niuxin Temple and was renamed in the Ming for the line about "clear sound." The second tier shows the whole scene: the two streams meeting, the bridges, the pavilion on its high point, the stone in the current. The third tier is the point: a monk crouches by the stream and says, "Don't rush off" and "Listen — the water is here."

The panel is a gentle correction to tick-box tourism. Qingyin Pavilion is not a place to photograph and leave. The sound changes with water volume, weather and season, and it is especially rich after rain. One minute on the bridge will teach you more about the site than another photo.

5. The Golden Summit: New Construction Is Also History

At the Golden Summit, the mountain changes again. At about 3,077 metres, it is the highest point of Mount Emei. Huazang Temple has been destroyed and rebuilt repeatedly, and most of the halls visible today are modern reconstructions. The striking Ten-Direction Samantabhadra statue was completed in 2006 and stands 48 metres tall.

Does that make the summit "less ancient"? Far from it. It shows that a living faith is not a museum specimen. It expresses itself through new buildings and new images. The summit has burned and been rebuilt before; what you see today is simply the latest link in that chain.

Huazang Temple and the ridge landscape frame each other, and together they make the point: to read a famous mountain, look at what earlier generations left — and at how later generations have continued it.

How to read this comic

The panel puts "ancient" and "contemporary" on the same page. At the top, craftsmen lay tiles on a roof frame, with the line "Roof tiles must be repaired generation after generation." Beside them hangs the plaque of the Great Hero Hall at Huazang Temple. In the middle stands the Ten-Direction Samantabhadra, labelled "completed 2006." Below are sunrise and a sea of clouds.

The message is clear: the summit is not a single ancient monument but the result of continuous maintenance and rebuilding. When you are there, separate the layers: which parts are old fabric, which are reconstructions, and which were added after 2006. Seeing the differences makes the tradition more impressive, not less.

6. The Four Wonders: Understanding the Buddha's Halo and the Holy Lamps

Sunrise, the sea of clouds, the Buddha's halo and the holy lamps are known as the four wonders of the Golden Summit. Sunrise and clouds are relatively easy to encounter. The halo and the lamps depend on conditions — and on how we choose to explain them.

The Buddha's halo: the shadow in the ring is yours

The Buddha's halo is an atmospheric optical phenomenon. With the sun behind you and cloud or fog in front, your shadow is projected onto the cloud. Light is reflected, diffracted and scattered by tiny water droplets, producing coloured rings around the shadow. The figure at the centre is not a deity; it is the observer. When conditions are right, each person in a group sees a halo centred on their own shadow.

Several conditions must line up: a low sun angle, sunlight behind the viewer, cloud or fog ahead, and a high viewpoint. Early morning and late afternoon are more likely, but this remains a phenomenon you cannot book. Seeing it is luck; not seeing it is normal.

The holy lamps: keep the record and the explanation apart

Historical sources also describe flickering lights in the valleys at night, called "holy lamps." What they were is still unresolved. Explanations include bioluminescence, moonlight reflected off water or mist, marsh gas, and effects of human vision. The safer approach is to separate what earlier observers recorded from the explanations later proposed. The record is history; the explanation is a hypothesis.

How to read this comic

The upper half shows the halo: a monk points at the cloud sea and says, "The ring surrounds the shadow," while a figure's shadow sits inside a coloured circle. The middle diagram makes the geometry explicit: the sun is behind the observer, the observer faces the cloud, the shadow falls on the cloud and the ring forms around it. The lower half shows the lamps: points of light in a valley, labelled "holy lamps (historical record)," with a scholar adding, "Keep the record and the explanation apart."

The lesson is not that the halo is "mystical," but that it can be explained. The lamps deserve the same discipline: do not rush to believe or dismiss. Describe what was seen first; discuss what it might have been second.

7. The Route: Walking Through the Layers

To walk the history in order, follow Baoguo Temple → Qingyin Pavilion → Wannian Temple → Golden Summit. The route climbs from the foot of the mountain to the top, and from landscape to old architecture to contemporary building.

Stop Layer Don't miss
Baoguo Temple Founded in the Ming, rebuilt later The gateway to the mountain and its overall layout
Qingyin Pavilion Formerly Niuxin Temple; renamed in the Ming The two waters, twin bridges, Niuxin Pavilion and the sound
Wannian Temple 399 origin; 980 bronze; 1600 brick hall The Song bronze, the Ming dome and the structural cutaway
Golden Summit Rebuilt over centuries; new statue in 2006 Samantabhadra, Huazang Temple, sunrise, clouds and the halo

Admission is about 160 yuan in peak season and 110 yuan in low season; cable cars and shuttle buses are extra. Opening times differ by site and season, so check the official notice. The summit is high and cold — bring a jacket even in summer.

How to split your time

Two days is the comfortable choice. On day one, go from Baoguo Temple to Qingyin Pavilion and Wannian Temple, focusing on the bronze, the brick hall and the landscape. On day two, go up to the Golden Summit for the statue, Huazang Temple and the four wonders. With only one day, prioritise Wannian Temple and the summit.

Frequently Asked Questions

When does the Buddhist history of Mount Emei begin?

Traditionally in 399, when the Eastern Jin monk Hui Chi entered Shu. Later gazetteers say he founded Puxian Temple, the predecessor of Wannian Temple.

How old is the bronze Samantabhadra in Wannian Temple?

It was cast in 980, during the Northern Song. It is about 7.35 metres tall and weighs around 62 tonnes, with Samantabhadra seated on a six-tusked white elephant.

Why is the Beamless Brick Hall called beamless?

Built in 1600, it uses brick vaults and a dome instead of a timber roof frame. "Beamless" means no wooden beams carry the structure, not that no wood is used at all.

Why is Mount Emei the sacred site of Samantabhadra?

Early worship, continuous teaching and the identification with the "Great Radiance Mountain" of scripture deepened the link. Imperial patronage and later temple building reinforced it over centuries.

Is the Golden Summit statue ancient?

No. The Ten-Direction Samantabhadra was completed in 2006 and stands 48 metres tall. Huazang Temple has also been rebuilt repeatedly, so most visible halls are modern.

How does the Buddha's halo form?

With the sun behind you and cloud in front, your shadow falls on the cloud. Water droplets reflect, diffract and scatter the light, creating coloured rings around the shadow. The figure inside is you.

Are the holy lamps real?

Historical records describe lights in the night valleys, but their cause is unresolved. Bioluminescence, moonlight, marsh gas and optical effects are hypotheses. Keep the record separate from the explanation.

How much is admission, and how should I plan?

About 160 yuan in peak season and 110 yuan in low season, with cable cars and shuttles extra; check the official notice. Allow two days: Baoguo, Qingyin and Wannian on day one, the Golden Summit on day two.

Conclusion: Reading a Mountain Means Reading Its Layers

What makes Mount Emei compelling is not one "thousand-year-old temple" but the way objects from different periods coexist: the temple site of the Eastern Jin, the bronze of the Northern Song, the brick hall of the Ming, repairs from the Qing, the new summit statue of 2006, and the maintenance still going on today. They stack like geological strata and grow like tree rings. Next time you stand under the dome at Wannian Temple, look at the statue first, then the hall, then the ceiling — and notice that you are standing in the middle of several eras at once.
