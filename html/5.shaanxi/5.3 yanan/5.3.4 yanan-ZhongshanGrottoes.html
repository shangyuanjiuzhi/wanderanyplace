Grottoes Overview

A comprehensive guide to the Zhongshan Grottoes — one of China's most exquisite Northern Song Dynasty Buddhist cave complexes, carved into the sandstone cliffs of Zhongshan Mountain from 1067 AD.

The Zhongshan Grottoes (钟山石窟), located in Zichang City north of Yan'an, began construction in 1067 AD during the Northern Song Dynasty and were hailed by historians as "the finest grottoes under heaven" — second only to Dunhuang. Of the entire complex, five caves remain open to visitors, but virtually all of the artistic essence is concentrated in Cave 3, the "Ten-Thousand-Buddha Rock" (万佛岩) — the other caves have suffered heavier weathering and can only be viewed from outside. A typical visit takes 1–2 hours.

What makes these grottoes extraordinary is the astonishing realism of the Song Dynasty sculptors. Rather than the idealized, remote Buddhas of earlier dynasties, the figures here are intensely human — veined hands, furrowed brows, and expressions caught mid-emotion. Carved directly into living sandstone and preserved for over 950 years, the statues offer an intimate window into the spiritual and artistic heights of medieval China.

Core Highlights

Five caves are open to the public, but all the essence is concentrated in Cave 3 — the "Ten-Thousand-Buddha Rock"; the other caves are heavily weathered and can only be viewed from the doorway. The images on the right show the statues and carvings inside the grottoes.

  • Three-Life Buddhas & Disciple Statues (三世佛与弟子像) — The Heart of Cave 3: Three groups of large freestanding sculptures on the central altar represent the Buddhas of the Past, Present and Future. The accompanying disciples — Kāśyapa (迦叶) and Ānanda (阿难) — are astonishingly lifelike: Kāśyapa's wrinkles and bulging veins are clearly visible, exemplifying the Song Dynasty sculptural ideal of "conveying the spirit through form" (以形传神) at its artistic peak.
  • Ten Thousand Buddhas in Array (万佛森列) — A Forest of Deities: Eight sandstone pillars and all four walls of the cave are blanketed with more than ten thousand small Buddha statues, packed densely together yet no two identical. The sheer scale and variety leave an overwhelming first impression — the moment most visitors fall silent.
  • Sixteen Arhats (十六罗汉) — Buddhas with Human Warmth: Along the base of the front wall, sixteen arhat figures each display a distinct personality, radiating the charm of everyday life. One particularly beloved statue shows an arhat whose earring has been bitten by a playful lion cub — his grimacing, pained expression is so vivid it still makes visitors smile nine centuries later.
  • Xuanzang's Pilgrimage Relief (玄奘取经图) — 500 Years Before "Journey to the West": Discovered in Cave 1, this stone carving depicts the monk Xuanzang's pilgrimage to India — created five centuries before the novel "Journey to the West" was written, making it the earliest surviving depiction of the pilgrimage and a treasure of national significance.
  • Three Teachings Unified (三教合一) — A Rare Religious Harmony: On the outer wall of the main cave, alongside the Buddhist statuary, stand Laozi (founder of Daoism) carved in the Ming Dynasty and Confucius (founder of Confucianism) added in the Qing Dynasty — a rare and remarkable visual testament to the fusion of Buddhism, Daoism and Confucianism in one sacred space.
